Rainbows

A rainbow isn't in a place. It's an angle: forty-two degrees from the point directly opposite the sun, which is why it moves when you move and why no two people ever see quite the same one. Every colour here is traced through a drop rather than chosen.

One rule: minimum deviation

A raindrop is a sphere, and parallel sunlight strikes every part of its face. Each ray bends on the way in, bounces off the back, bends again on the way out, and leaves having been turned through some total angle. The angle depends on where the ray struck, and the interesting thing is how: as you move the entry point out from the centre the exit angle climbs, slows, stalls and comes back down. There is a maximum, and near it a whole span of entry points leave in almost exactly the same direction.

That stall is the rainbow. Light leaving in all other directions is spread thin; light leaving near the turning point is concentrated, because a wide band of the drop's face is funnelled into a narrow range of angles. Watch the fan of rays in the drop panel bunch up as you drag through the rainbow ray, and watch the curve in the next panel go flat. No drop is doing anything special, and no drop is at the rainbow: every drop scatters the same way, and the ones you see lit are simply those sitting at the right angle from your eye.

That angle is 42° from the antisolar point for red and about 40.7° for violet, because water bends violet slightly more. Red on the outside. Nothing in that depends on where the drops are, which is why a rainbow is a property of your position rather than of the shower, why it retreats as you walk towards it, and why the person beside you is seeing a different one built from different drops.

The second bow, and the dark band between

Some light takes a second bounce inside the drop before leaving. Run that through the same argument and you get another turning point, this time at 50° for red and 53.4° for violet, so the secondary bow is wider, fainter, and its colours are the other way round. Set the reflections control to two and watch the curve flip.

Between them sits the best-kept secret in the sky. The primary sends light anywhere from straight back at you out to 42° and no further; the secondary sends light from 50° outwards and no closer. Between 42° and 50° neither bow delivers anything at all, so that ring of sky is genuinely darker than the sky inside the primary or outside the secondary. It's called Alexander's dark band, after the Greek who noticed it eighteen centuries ago, and the deviation panel shows exactly why it's empty.

A third bounce gives a third bow, and it lands 43° from the sun rather than from the antisolar point, which is to say behind you in the glare. It was photographed for the first time in 2011.

Why you never see one at midday in summer

The antisolar point sits as far below the horizon as the sun is above it, so the top of the bow reaches 42° minus the sun's altitude. Once the sun climbs past 42° the whole primary is underground, and past 50° the secondary goes too. At 53° N the sun clears 42° in the middle of the day from late April to mid-August, which rules out midday rainbows for four months of the year; from the tropics they are a morning and evening business all year round. The flipside is that a sunset rainbow is nearly a full semicircle and stands at its tallest, and from a cliff or an aeroplane you can see the circle close below the horizon.

The same trick in ice

Swap the sphere for an ice crystal and the argument survives: a hexagonal column has 60° prism faces, and light passing through them also has a minimum deviation, at 21.7° for red and 22.3° for violet. That is the 22° halo you see ringing the sun through thin cloud, and because the minimum is a minimum rather than a maximum the colours run the other way, red on the inside. The 46° halo is the same crystal used through its 90° corner. That deserves its own page.

Small print

Rays are traced with Snell's law and weighted by the Fresnel coefficients for unpolarised light, sampled evenly across the drop's face and histogrammed by exit angle, so the brightness profile and the width of each bow come out of the model rather than being drawn. Water's dispersion is the standard fit, about 1.343 in the violet to 1.330 in the red; the index slider adds an offset to the whole curve. Colours are the resulting spectrum at each angle, run through the CIE 1931 functions into sRGB and normalised for brightness, which is why they look pastel: every angle receives a broad band of wavelengths, and a real rainbow is much less saturated than a painted one. This is geometric optics only, so the supernumerary fringes just inside the primary, which are an interference effect and need drop size and wave theory, are absent. The sun is treated as a point; its half-degree width blurs each bow by that much.
