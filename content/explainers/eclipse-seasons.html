Eclipse seasons

There is a new moon every month and a full moon every month, yet eclipses come in pairs twice a year. The moon's orbit is tilted five degrees, so most months its shadow passes above or below us. Only when the line where the two planes cross happens to point at the sun can anything line up.

Why not every month

If the moon's orbit lay flat in the ecliptic, every new moon would be a solar eclipse and every full moon a lunar one: twenty-four or twenty-five a year, most of them total. Set the tilt slider to zero and that is exactly what happens. The five-degree tilt ruins it. Five degrees is ten times the width of the sun, so at a typical new moon the moon's shadow passes a few hundred thousand kilometres above or below Earth and nothing happens at all.

The orbit still has to cross the ecliptic somewhere, and being a circle it crosses twice, at two opposite points called the nodes. Only there is the moon in the same plane as the sun and Earth. So an eclipse needs two coincidences at once: the moon must be new or full, and it must be within a few degrees of a node. The first happens twice a month, the second twice a year, and eclipses are what you get where the two agree.

Seasons, and why they drift

The line of nodes stays put in space while Earth goes round the sun, so twice a year the sun lines up with it. Each alignment opens a window about a month long, which is why eclipses arrive in pairs or threes: a solar eclipse at the new moon and a lunar one at the full moon a fortnight either side, sometimes with a second solar eclipse at the far edge of the window.

If that were the whole story the seasons would sit at the same calendar dates forever. They don't, because the sun and moon tug the orbit around, dragging the line of nodes backwards at about nineteen and a third degrees a year and all the way round in 18.6 years. Earth has to catch a node that is coming to meet it, so it takes only 346.6 days rather than a year: an eclipse year. Each season therefore falls about nineteen days earlier in the calendar than the last, which is the slant you see in the twenty-year chart. Set the node drift to zero and the slant disappears; reverse it and the seasons march the other way.

Eighteen years and eleven days later, the moon has completed a whole number of months of all three kinds at once: 223 back to new moon, 242 back to the same node, 239 back to the same distance. So the geometry repeats and the eclipse comes again, a third of the way round the world. That is the saros, and jumping the year slider on by eighteen puts almost the same eclipse eleven days later in the calendar.

What this adds to the harvest moon

The harvest moon page treats the moon as riding exactly on the ecliptic, which is the simplification this page is about. The same five degrees that create eclipse seasons also lift and lower the moon's declination by up to five degrees beyond the sun's range, on the same 18.6-year cycle. When the ascending node sits near the March equinox point, the winter full moon climbs to 28.7° rather than 23.4° and rides higher than any sun ever does. That is a major standstill, and the last one was in 2024 and 2025.

Small print

The sun and moon come from mean orbital elements with the main periodic terms, which puts the moments of new and full moon within a few minutes and the moon's latitude within a hundredth of a degree. Over 2026 to 2028 that reproduces the real eclipse record exactly, dates and types alike, though a grazing penumbral eclipse can fall either side of the line. Whether an eclipse is total or annular is decided by comparing the two discs at that moment; where on Earth it is visible, and the difference between a central eclipse and one that only grazes the pole, would need the full geometry of Earth's globe and is not modelled. Changing the tilt or the node drift leaves the rest of the model alone, so those settings answer "what if" rather than describing any real body.
