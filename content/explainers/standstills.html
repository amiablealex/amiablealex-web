The moon's eighteen-year swing

Every month the moon swings north of the equator and back south, the way the sun does over a year. How far it swings is not fixed. Over 18.6 years its reach grows to 28.6 degrees, further than the sun ever goes, then shrinks to 18.3, well short of it. The two ends are the major and minor lunar standstills, and they come from two tilts taking turns to add and cancel: Earth's 23.4 degrees, and the moon's own five.

Two tilts, taking turns

The moon's monthly path is the ecliptic with a five-degree tilt of its own laid on top. The ecliptic itself, the sun's road round the sky, is tilted 23.44 degrees to the equator, and that tilt has a fixed high side: the ecliptic climbs furthest north at the point the sun reaches in June, and sinks furthest south opposite it, at the December point. The moon's path is tilted 5.14 degrees to the ecliptic, so it has a high side too. The difference is that the moon's high side does not stay put. The tilt keeps its size but swings round, the way a plate wobbling on a table keeps the same slope while its highest edge walks round the rim.

Everything on this page comes down to where those two high sides are pointing. When they point the same way, the moon's path rides five degrees above the ecliptic exactly where the ecliptic is already highest, and the two stack: 23.44 + 5.14 = 28.6 degrees. That is a major standstill. Nine years on, the moon's high side has swung round to face the other way, its path runs five degrees below the ecliptic exactly where the ecliptic peaks, and the reach falls to 23.44 − 5.14 = 18.3. That is a minor one. Halfway between, with the high sides at right angles, the moon's tilt is spent lifting its path where the ecliptic is crossing the equator rather than where it peaks. The two tilts then combine like the sides of a right-angled triangle, and the reach is 24.0 degrees, not Earth's 23.4 alone.

The second panel is the plainest way to see it. Unroll the sky into a strip and the ecliptic becomes a wave 23.4 degrees high; the moon's extra tilt is a second, smaller wave added on top. Crest on crest and they add; crest on trough and they cancel. It is the same arithmetic as spring and neap tides, run on a far slower clock.

Why the high side goes round

The moon's orbit is not left alone. The sun pulls on it, and because the orbit is tilted, that pull keeps trying to drag it flat into the ecliptic. A tilted, spinning thing pushed like that does not flatten: it swings sideways instead, like a top that leans and circles rather than falling over. So the tilt stays at five degrees while the line where the two planes cross, the line of nodes, creeps backwards round the sky, once every 18.61 years.

That is the same line of nodes that decides eclipse seasons. There it is measured against the sun, which comes round to it twice a year. Here it is measured against the fixed points of the ecliptic, the equinoxes and solstices, and it takes 18.6 years to get round them. Which is why the date barely matters on this page: the moon runs the whole ecliptic every month, so every month carries the full swing. What matters is the year.

The nodes' position decides the rest. With the node where the moon crosses northwards sitting at the March equinox point, the moon's highest stretch lands on the June point and the tilts add. With that node at the September point, they cancel. With the nodes at the solstices, you get the halfway case.

Why it is called a standstill

The sun's rising point creeps north along the horizon through spring, slows, stops for a few days around midsummer and turns back. That pause gave us the word solstice: the sun standing still. The moon does the same every month, swinging from its northernmost rising point to its southernmost and back in four weeks. And those monthly extremes have their own, far slower swing: year by year they push further out, or draw further in, as the nodes go round. Near the two ends of the cycle the change all but stops, and for a year or two the moon's furthest rising points sit in the same places month after month. Those are the standstills — the moon's solstices, on an eighteen-year clock.

Higher than the sun, lower than the sun

From the UK at 53 degrees north, the midsummer sun peaks at 60 degrees. During a major standstill the moon at its northern extreme climbs to 65.6, higher than the sun ever goes, and a fortnight later at its southern extreme it scrapes along at 8.4. Its rising point swings with it: further round to the north-east than any sunrise one week, further to the south-east than any sunrise the next. At a minor standstill everything pulls in: the moon tops out at 55 degrees and never sinks below 19.

Which phase is doing the climbing depends on the season, by the rule on the harvest moon page. The full moon sits opposite the sun, so around midwinter it is the full moon that rides the moon's northern extreme. In a major-standstill winter, the full moon over Britain stands higher than any midsummer sun. In a major-standstill summer, it is the full moon that hugs the southern horizon.

Go further north and high and low turn into something stronger. At Tromsø, 69.6 degrees north, a major standstill keeps the moon above the horizon for nearly a week at a time each month, and below it for nearly a week a fortnight later; at a minor standstill it rises and sets every day like everything else. Go south instead and the moon passes straight overhead anywhere within 28.6 degrees of the equator at a major standstill, five degrees further out than the sun ever reaches.

The people who noticed

Anyone watching moonrise against the same horizon for a couple of decades would see the swing, and some of the people who built in stone seem to have done exactly that. At Callanish on Lewis, at 58 degrees north, the southern moon of a major standstill clears the horizon by only about three degrees. It rolls low along the hills to the south, the line locally likened to a sleeping woman, and from parts of the site it seems to set among the stones. Alexander Thom and others argued the layout was built for it. How deliberate it was is still argued over, and the stones were back in the news for the 2024 and 2025 standstill.

The firmest case is in Colorado. At Chimney Rock, a Chacoan great house sits high on a ridge, and from it the moon at its northern standstill extreme rises in the gap between two stone pinnacles, which the sun never does from that spot. Tree rings in its timbers date two building phases to about AD 1076 and 1093, both standstill years. Stonehenge has carried a standstill theory since the 1960s; that one is still being argued over.

What else swings with it

The moon's reach turns up across the rest of the site. On the tides page, the latitude above which the second daily high water disappears is ninety degrees minus the moon's declination, the same line beyond which the moon stops setting, so over the cycle it wanders between about 61 and 72 degrees and the coast in between swaps two tides a day for one and back. The harvest moon page sets the moon on the ecliptic for simplicity; in a major-standstill year the real September full moon does even better than that page shows, because the moon's path then meets the eastern horizon at an even shallower angle than the ecliptic does. And on eclipse seasons, the same sliding line of nodes is what moves the eclipses nineteen days earlier each year.

Small print

The moon's path here is the mean orbit: a circle on the sky tilted 5.145° to the ecliptic, with its nodes sliding backwards at the steady mean rate of 19.35° a year. That puts the major standstills in June 2006, January 2025 and September 2043 and the minor ones in 1997, 2015 and 2034, within a few weeks of the dates worked out from the true, wobbling node. The moon's place along its path is real, from mean elements with the main periodic terms, but its height above the ecliptic comes from the mean circle, so the moon always sits on the drawn path; the real one strays a few tenths of a degree either side of it. The real tilt also nods by about 0.15° on a 173-day beat as the sun's pull on it changes, which is why published maximum declinations at a major standstill run to 28.7° rather than 28.6°. Altitudes are geometric, for the centre of the moon as seen from the centre of Earth: refraction lifts the real moon by about half a degree at the horizon, and parallax lowers it by up to a degree, because the moon is near enough that where you stand on Earth makes a difference. Earth's tilt is held at 23.44°; it is slowly shrinking, on a clock that has a page of its own.
