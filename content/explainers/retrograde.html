Retrograde motion

Every couple of years Mars stops dead against the stars, reverses for ten weeks, stops again and carries on. Nothing happens to Mars. We overtake it on the inside, and for a while the direction we're looking swings backwards. This is the observation that broke the geocentric model.

Overtaking on the inside

Planets closer to the sun move faster, both because the sun's pull is stronger there and because they have less distance to cover. Earth takes a year; Mars takes 687 days. So every 780 days Earth catches Mars up and sweeps past it on the inside, the way a car in the inner lane overtakes one in the outer. During the overtaking, the direction from us to Mars swings backwards, and since we measure a planet's position as a direction against the fixed stars, Mars appears to reverse.

Watch the sight-line in the first panel as you play the date forward. Most of the time Earth and the planet are on unrelated parts of their orbits and the line sweeps steadily one way. As Earth comes level, the line pivots, holds still for a day or two, and tracks back. That pause is the stationary point, and it is why the ancients described planets as standing still.

The tell that this is our motion and not theirs is where it happens. An outer planet only goes retrograde at opposition, when it is directly opposite the sun, closest to us, brightest, and visible all night. Venus and Mercury only go retrograde at inferior conjunction, when they are between us and the sun, invisible in the glare. In a geocentric model those are two unrelated coincidences. In a heliocentric one they are the same fact: retrograde is the moment of closest approach, and which side of us the planet passes on decides whether that moment happens in the midnight sky or the noon one.

What Ptolemy had to build

To reproduce this without moving the Earth you need epicycles: each planet rides a small circle whose centre rides the big one. It works, and it fitted the observations for fourteen centuries. But there is a detail that should have itched. For every outer planet, the epicycle takes exactly one year to go round, no matter how long the planet's own circuit takes. Mars, Jupiter and Saturn share a period that belongs to none of them. In the heliocentric picture that shared year has an obvious owner: it is ours. The epicycle was Earth's own orbit, reflected into every planet's motion because every observation was made from a moving platform.

Loops, zigzags and the size of the wiggle

The apparent path only makes a loop when the planet has some ecliptic latitude to work with. Orbits are tilted by a degree or two, so a planet that happens to be off the ecliptic at opposition traces a proper loop, and one that is crossing a node traces a flat zigzag instead. The same planet does both at different oppositions, which is why no two retrogrades look alike.

The size of the backward arc depends on how much nearer we are than the planet. Mars is close, so it backs up ten to twenty degrees and does it briskly, in about ten weeks. Saturn, nine times further out, barely notices us going past: it backs up around seven degrees and takes four and a half months about it. Mars' arc varies far more than the others between oppositions, because its orbit is eccentric enough that some passes are much closer than others.

Small print

Positions come from the standard Keplerian elements with their per-century rates, solved through Kepler's equation, which places the planets to within a fraction of a degree over the decades shown here. The model reproduces the real retrograde windows: Mars from 7 December 2024 to 24 February 2025, Jupiter from 9 October 2024 to 4 February 2025, Venus from 2 March to 13 April 2025. Positions are geometric and heliocentric-to-geocentric only: no light travel time, no aberration, no precession of the equinox, and the "stars" are a fixed ecliptic frame. Dates use the ecliptic longitude of date without nutation. Distances are in astronomical units, measured from Earth.
