Three wobbles, three jobs

Tilt is the simplest. More tilt means fiercer seasons everywhere, summers hotter and winters colder in both hemispheres at once, and the poles gain most because tilt is what gives them a summer at all. It swings between about 22.1° and 24.5° every 41,000 years. We are at 23.44° and falling, nine thousand years past the last maximum.

Precession is the sly one. It doesn't change how much sunlight Earth gets in a year, or even how much any latitude gets in a year. It only moves which season happens at the near end of the orbit. Today perihelion falls on 3 January, so the northern hemisphere takes its winter close to the sun and its summer far away: mild winters, cool summers. Eleven thousand years from now that is reversed and it is the southern hemisphere's turn. Both hemispheres are always on opposite ends of this deal, which is why an ice age driven by precession alone would be a northern and southern see-saw rather than a global event.

Eccentricity does almost nothing by itself. Even at its most elliptical the orbit changes the year's total sunlight by well under a percent. What it does is set the gain on precession: with a near-circular orbit it makes no difference at all which season sits at perihelion, and the precession signal vanishes. Watch the summer curve with eccentricity near zero, around 28,000 years from now, and the 21,000-year wiggles flatten right out.

Why 65° north

Ice sheets don't grow because winters are cold; they are always cold enough to snow. They grow when summers are too cool to melt what fell. That makes summer sunlight at the latitude of the ice, around 65° north where Canada and Scandinavia sit, the number to watch. Today it is 478 watts per square metre at the June solstice. At the last glacial maximum it was 462, and eleven thousand years ago, when the ice sheets collapsed, it was 539.

Set the latitude to the equator and the curve nearly flattens: the tropics barely notice any of this. Set it to 65° south and it inverts, because that hemisphere's summer is at the other end of the orbit. Set the day selector to the whole year and every precession wiggle disappears: what a latitude receives over a whole year depends on tilt and, very weakly, on eccentricity, but not at all on which season sits at perihelion. Switch to perihelion only with the whole year showing and the line is dead flat. That is the real lesson. Milankovitch is not about how much sunlight Earth receives, but about when and where it lands.

What the theory doesn't explain

The honest part. Over the last million years the ice ages have come and gone roughly every 100,000 years, matching the eccentricity cycle. But eccentricity is by far the weakest of the three forcings, and the insolation curve on this page is dominated by the 21,000-year precession beat with a 41,000-year tilt beat behind it. The 100,000-year rhythm in the ice is much stronger than the 100,000-year rhythm in the sunlight, and before about a million years ago the ice followed the 41,000-year tilt beat instead and then switched. Something in the ice sheets, the carbon cycle and the oceans is amplifying a weak nudge and picking which beat to dance to. The orbital clock sets the timing; it does not supply the power.

Small print

Daily insolation is the standard expression, integrating the solar elevation over the day with the inverse-square distance from Kepler's orbit, at a solar constant of 1361 watts per square metre; it reproduces the textbook 478 for 65° N at today's June solstice. The orbital history is idealised: one 41,000-year term for tilt, a steady 21,700-year circuit for perihelion, and 100,000 and 405,000-year terms for eccentricity, each with the right range and phased to match today's values exactly. That makes the shape, the interplay and the recent few cycles faithful, and it drifts from the full astronomical solution deeper into the past, where the real terms beat against each other. Dates are given as years from now. "Perihelion falls on" uses today's calendar as a convenience; the calendar itself has no meaning over these spans, only the season does.
