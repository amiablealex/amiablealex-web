The morning star

Venus is the brightest thing in the sky after the sun and moon, and you can only ever see it near dawn or dusk. It is never overhead at midnight, never opposite the sun, never more than about three hours from one horizon or the other. That restriction is pure geometry, and it comes with a bonus: Venus has phases, they run backwards from what you would guess, and one of them killed a fourteen-hundred-year-old model of the universe.

Tied to the sun by geometry

Venus orbits closer to the sun than we do, at 0.72 of our distance. Draw a line from Earth that just grazes its orbit and you have the furthest from the sun it can ever appear, and the angle is simply the inverse sine of 0.72, which is 46 degrees. Its orbit is slightly lopsided, so in practice the greatest elongations run between about 45 and 47 degrees.

Forty-six degrees is roughly three hours of sky rotation. So Venus rises at most three hours before the sun, or sets at most three hours after it, and there is no possible arrangement of the solar system in which you see it at midnight. Every ancient culture that watched the sky noticed this, and several concluded there were two objects rather than one: a morning star and an evening star. The Greeks called them Phosphorus and Hesperus and it took them a while to work out they were the same thing.

Mercury is worse off still, at 0.39 of our distance, giving a limit of 23 degrees. That is why most people have never knowingly seen it, and why Copernicus is said to have died without ever having done so.

A good apparition and a bad one

The 46 degrees is measured along the ecliptic, the line the sun and planets follow through the sky. What you actually see is height above your horizon, and the exchange rate between the two is not fixed: it depends on the angle the ecliptic makes with the horizon at the moment you are looking, and that changes through the year and with your latitude. The same 46 degrees can put Venus 40 degrees up, or barely 10.

From northern latitudes the ecliptic stands steepest against the western horizon after sunset in early spring, and steepest against the eastern horizon before sunrise in early autumn. So the best evening apparitions are the ones peaking around March and April, and the best morning ones around September and October; an evening apparition peaking in September is the same 46 degrees and hardly worth the trouble. Nearer the equator the ecliptic is steep all year and every apparition is a good one, which is part of why Venus mattered so much to civilisations in the tropics. The phases run backwards

Venus goes through phases like the moon, and Galileo was the first to see them, in 1610. But they behave in a way that catches people out. When Venus is full, it is on the far side of the sun from us, nearly two astronomical units away, so it is tiny — about ten arcseconds across. When it is a thin crescent it is on our side of the sun, six times closer, and about sixty arcseconds across. The phase and the size are locked together and they pull in opposite directions.

So which arrangement is brightest? Multiply the illuminated area by the inverse square of the distance and the answer comes out at neither end. Venus is at its most brilliant as a fat crescent about a quarter lit, roughly 39 degrees from the sun and about five weeks either side of passing between us and the sun. The cycle panel shows the two curves crossing and the brightness peaking between them. At that moment it reaches magnitude −4.8, bright enough to cast a shadow on snow, and bright enough that it is the single most common object reported as a UFO.

The observation that broke the old model

Here is why the phases mattered so much. In Ptolemy's system, which stood for fourteen centuries, Venus rode on an epicycle whose centre was fixed on the line between Earth and the sun, always on our side of it. That arrangement can reproduce the elongation limit perfectly well, which is why it survived so long. But it has a consequence its authors never had the means to check: if Venus is always between us and the sun, its lit face is always mostly turned away, and it can only ever show us crescents and thin ones at that.

Galileo pointed a telescope at it and saw a full Venus, and a gibbous one, and a small one when it was full. There is no arrangement of epicycles in front of the sun that produces that. Venus must pass behind the sun as well as in front of it, which means it goes round the sun rather than round us. One observation, and the model was finished. It is a rare case of a single measurement being genuinely fatal, and it pairs with the epicycle problem described on the retrograde page.

Five points in eight years

This is also the answer to the odd looping motion you get by playing the years on the first panel. Venus climbs out to one side of the sun, swings back through it, climbs out the other side, and repeats — but each apparition happens with Earth somewhere different in its orbit, so the tilt of the ecliptic against your horizon is different each time and the same 46 degrees of elongation buys a different altitude. Five apparitions later it starts repeating. Venus passes between us and the sun every 584 days. Eight of our years are 2,922 days and five of those passes are 2,920, which is a coincidence of arithmetic with no deep cause behind it, but a very tidy one. It means that after eight years Venus returns to almost exactly the same place in the sky at almost exactly the same date, two days early.

But why should that put Venus back in the same part of the sky? Because of what an inferior conjunction is. At that moment Venus lies directly between us and the sun, so the direction from Earth to Venus is the same as the direction from Earth to the sun — and that direction depends on nothing except where Earth is in its own orbit. The place on the ecliptic where a pass happens is simply Earth's position at the time. That is what the crosses in the last panel are: where Earth stood each time Venus lapped it, with the spoke running out to the longitude it marked. Five passes take Earth almost exactly eight times round, so on the sixth it is standing within a couple of degrees of the first cross, pointing at the same stars.

Each pass happens about 144 degrees round the ecliptic from the last, so joining five successive passes in order draws a five-pointed star. The last panel plots them. Over decades the figure slowly rotates, because those two days a cycle accumulate, but for a human lifetime it is a near-perfect pentagram written on the sky by a planet. The Maya built calendar cycles around the 584-day period, and were tracking it accurately enough to predict Venus's reappearance to within a couple of hours.

The pass that usually misses

Venus passes between us and the sun five times in eight years, and almost every time it misses. Its orbit is tilted 3.4 degrees to ours, so at a pass it usually sits a little north or south of the sun rather than on it — from 0.28 AU away that is up to about eight degrees, sixteen times the width of the sun itself. The two points where the orbits cross are the only places a pass can land on the disc, and Earth goes past that line in early June and early December. It is the same node geometry that decides when eclipses can happen, running on a 584-day period instead of a monthly one.

When a pass does land near a node you get a transit of Venus: a black dot crossing the sun for six hours. Because the eight-year cycle so nearly repeats, transits come in pairs eight years apart, and then the drift carries the pattern off the node for either 105 or 121 years. The last pair was 2004 and 2012. The next is December 2117. Halley worked out in 1716 that timing a transit from widely separated places would give the true scale of the solar system, and the expeditions sent out for the 1761 and 1769 pair — Cook's voyage to Tahiti among them — were the first international scientific collaboration on that scale.

Small print

Positions come from the standard Keplerian elements with their per-century rates, solved through Kepler's equation, the same source the retrograde page uses. Phase angle, lit fraction and distance follow from the triangle of sun, Venus and Earth. Apparent diameter takes Venus as 12,104 km across; brightness uses the standard magnitude expression with its phase-angle terms and reproduces the accepted peak near magnitude −4.8. The twilight panel places Venus at the moment the sun is six degrees below the horizon, computing both positions properly for the date and latitude, but ignoring refraction and treating the sky as clear. Greatest elongations in this model run between 45 and 47 degrees, matching the real spread, and at greatest elongation the model returns Venus exactly half lit, which is the geometric signature of the sun-Venus-Earth angle being a right angle. In the first panel the disc is magnified about two thousand times and fades out below fifteen degrees of altitude, where haze and the long air path would really take it. The pentagram is drawn from the actual inferior conjunctions the model finds, and it picks them out by distance as well as by elongation, because Venus also passes nearly in line with the sun when it is on the far side, 1.7 AU away and full. Only the near passes, at about 0.28 AU, count. The dates it produces for the current cycle are 25 October 2026, 1 June 2028, 6 January 2030, 11 August 2031 and 21 March 2033, which are the real ones. The month labels on that ring are the sun's own longitude on the first of each month, from the same model, which is what makes the ring a calendar rather than a coordinate.
