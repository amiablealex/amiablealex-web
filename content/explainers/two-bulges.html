Nearer is stronger

Gravity weakens with distance, so the moon pulls the near side of Earth harder than it pulls the middle, and the middle harder than it pulls the far side. That single sloping fact is the whole of the tide. The near water runs ahead, the far water is left behind, and Earth comes out stretched along the line to the moon — with a bulge at both ends.

From outside you see three pulls that differ by three per cent and a whole lot of falling that everything does together. Ride along with the middle and the falling cancels, leaving the three per cent on its own.

This switch drives the panel above and the one below. Measuring from Earth's centre means subtracting the pull Earth itself feels — which is exactly what falling freely does for you, at no charge.

The slope is everything

Newton's law puts distance in the denominator, squared, so the strength of a pull depends on how far away you are standing. Earth is 12,742 km thick, and across that thickness the moon's grip changes measurably: 34.3 millionths of a metre per second squared on the near side, 33.2 in the middle, 32.1 on the far side. A three per cent slope from one side of the planet to the other. Everything on this page is that slope and nothing else.

Three per cent sounds like nothing, and as a pull it is. But a pull is not what an ocean can feel. Earth as a body is falling towards the moon, and everything falling together feels nothing at all — that is what weightlessness is. The 33.2 in the middle is the part everybody gets, and the part nobody notices. What is left over is the 1.1 on the near side and the −1.1 on the far side, and the leftovers are the only thing with anywhere to push.

Which is why the far bulge is not the puzzle it looks. The moon does not pull the far water away from Earth. It pulls Earth away from the far water, and the far water, lagging, ends up standing proud. Come back to the falling panel and let it run for a day: Earth's centre covers 124 kilometres, the near water 128 and the far water 120. The four kilometres at each end is the entire tide. The 124 in the middle does nothing, because everything does it together.

Around the sides the slope shows up as a turn rather than a stretch. Water at the flanks is aimed very slightly inwards, because each parcel points at the moon itself rather than straight down the axis, and the angle between those two directions is Earth's radius over the moon's distance. That leftover squeezes towards the axis at exactly half the strength of the stretch along it. Stretch one way, squeeze the other two, and the water has nowhere to go but the two ends.

Is it centrifugal?

You will often read that the far bulge is flung outwards by Earth's swing around the Earth–moon barycentre. That is not quite a myth, but it is a detour, and it is usually told wrongly. Done carefully, in the frame revolving with the two bodies, the inertial term is exactly the same size and direction at every point of Earth, because in a revolution without rotation every point traces an identical circle. Subtract that uniform term from the moon's non-uniform pull and you get the two bulges — which is the same arithmetic as subtracting the pull at Earth's centre, because that is precisely what the uniform term equals. It is the same switch as the one on the panels above, arrived at by a longer road.

The trouble is what people picture when they hear the word. They imagine something that varies across Earth, or they blame Earth's daily spin, and then they are explaining a real effect with a mechanism that is not doing the work. The panel at the top of this page is the test that settles it. Nothing in it is orbiting and nothing is spinning: the moon sits still, everything is dropped from rest, and both bulges appear anyway. The stretch comes from the pull being uneven, not from going round anything.

A ten-millionth of gravity, moving an ocean

What is left after the subtraction is pitiful. The moon's pull on you is about three hundredths of a millimetre per second squared; the leftover, after the shared part is gone, is a thousandth of a millimetre per second squared. That is roughly one ten-millionth of Earth's own gravity. It could never lift water. Drop a pebble and the moon changes where it lands by less than the width of an atom.

What saves it is direction. Almost everywhere on Earth the leftover does not point up or down but along the surface, and sideways is the one direction Earth's gravity does not resist. A force that could not lift a teaspoon can slide a whole ocean downhill towards the two ends of the axis, given six hours and no opposition. The bulge is not water being lifted. It is water arriving.

Two bulges standing still while Earth turns underneath gives you two high waters a day, fifty minutes later each time. The sun raises a second, smaller pair of its own, and where the two sets line up decides whether this fortnight's tides are big or feeble. And the same egg, raised in rock rather than water, is what eventually stopped the moon turning.

Small print

The slope panel plots the moon's pull at every point along the Earth–moon line across Earth's diameter, with nothing exaggerated: the flatness of the line when it is measured from outside is real, and so is the thirteenfold shrink of the axis needed to see what survives the subtraction. The arrow panel does the same subtraction at sixteen points on the surface, and states its own magnification as you switch frames. The falling panel uses the moon's mean distance, 384,400 km, and Earth's radius, 6,371 km, with the moon held fixed and every parcel released from rest. Displacements are computed from the real accelerations and then magnified against Earth's size by the factor shown, because at true scale a day of falling moves everything about one fiftieth of an Earth radius and the difference between the parcels is a thirtieth of that again. Within each view the same magnification applies to the falling and to the stretching, so the one really is the small fraction of the other that it looks; the two views use different factors, and each says which. The arrow panel subtracts the pull at Earth's centre from the pull at sixteen points on the surface, and states its own magnification as you step through it. Everything here is the tide-raising force, not the tide: what an ocean does with it is another matter, and a local one.
