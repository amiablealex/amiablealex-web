The analemma

Every other page in this set quietly used solar time, where noon is whenever the sun is highest. Clocks don't work like that. They tick out an average day, and the real sun runs up to sixteen minutes ahead of them and fourteen behind. Photograph it at the same clock time all year and it traces a figure of eight.

Why clocks and the sun disagree

A clock's day is exactly 24 hours, every day. The sun's day, from one noon to the next, is not: it runs as much as half a minute long or short, and those half-minutes accumulate into a discrepancy of a quarter of an hour either way. Two separate causes are at work, and they have nothing to do with each other.

The first is the tilt, and it is the same effect that gave us the harvest moon. The sun moves at a steady pace along the ecliptic, but a clock keeps time with the spinning Earth, which is to say with the equator. Near the equinox points the ecliptic crosses the equator at an angle, so a day's worth of motion along it projects onto a smaller step in right ascension and the sun falls behind; near the solstice points the ecliptic runs parallel to the equator, the same step projects longer, and the sun runs ahead. That gives a wave with two full cycles a year, worth about ten minutes each way, which is zero at the equinoxes and solstices and extreme halfway between. Set eccentricity to zero and it's all that remains, and the analemma becomes a perfectly symmetrical figure of eight.

The second is the ellipse. Earth is closest to the sun in early January and moves fastest then, so the sun appears to race along the ecliptic in January and dawdle in July. That gives one cycle a year, worth about eight minutes each way, zero at perihelion and aphelion. Set the tilt to zero and only this remains: the declination never changes, so the analemma collapses to a horizontal line that the sun slides back and forth along.

Add the ten-minute double wave to the eight-minute single wave and you get the lopsided curve that peaks at sixteen minutes in early November and bottoms at fourteen in February. The analemma is lopsided for the same reason, with a small summer loop and a large winter one. Drag perihelion round to March and watch the two loops even out.

The thing you actually notice

In mid-December the evenings start drawing out before the shortest day arrives, and the mornings keep getting darker for another week after it. Nothing is wrong: sunset and sunrise are each the sum of two things, half the day length and the position of solar noon. Around the solstice the first has stalled, changing by seconds a day, while the second is still sliding half a minute a day as the equation of time runs towards its December zero. That slide wins, so the earliest sunset arrives about a week early and the latest sunrise about a week late, while the shortest day sits exactly at the solstice between them. Push the latitude towards the equator, where the day length barely moves at all, and the gap widens to weeks. Push it towards the pole, where day length changes violently, and the three dates close up on each other.

All of this is what the earlier pages left out when they said "solar time". Everything here is in local mean time instead: a clock that keeps the average day and reads 12:00 at the average noon. Your own clock differs from it by a fixed amount and nothing more, so none of the shapes or dates on this page would change — four minutes for every degree you sit west of your time zone's meridian, plus an hour when summer time is in force. That is also why the photograph slider covers it: shifting the clock time by four minutes is the same as moving a degree of longitude, and summer time is just photographing an hour earlier.

Small print

The orbit is solved from Kepler's equation rather than a series, so the exaggerated eccentricities stay exact. Perihelion moves the orbit while the March equinox stays pinned to its 2026 date, so the seasons keep their calendar. Rise and set are geometric, for the centre of the sun with no atmospheric refraction, which in reality adds a few minutes at each end and shifts the three December dates by a day or so. Times are local mean time throughout; to reach a real clock, add four minutes for every degree west of your zone's meridian and an hour for summer time.
