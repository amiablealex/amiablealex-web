The colour of the aurora

Green and red aurora come from the same atom. What separates them is time: the red light takes nearly two minutes to leave the atom, so it can only escape where the air is thin enough that nothing bumps into it first. The colours you see are a direct readout of how crowded the air is, painted across the sky.

One atom, two colours, and a stopwatch

Almost all of the aurora is oxygen, and the two famous colours come from the same oxygen atom in two different excited states. An electron from space slams into it, knocks it into one of those states, and the atom then has to shed the energy as light. The question is whether it gets the chance.

The green line at 558 nanometres comes from a state that takes about three quarters of a second to emit. The red line at 630 nanometres comes from a state that takes around a hundred and ten seconds. Both are what physicists call forbidden transitions, meaning they are so unlikely that the atom sits there holding the energy for an absurdly long time by atomic standards, where an ordinary transition is over in a hundred-millionth of a second.

That delay is the whole story. If another molecule collides with the atom while it is still holding the energy, the energy goes into the collision instead and no photon is ever made. So each colour can only exist where the air is thin enough to leave the atom alone for long enough. Below about ninety-five kilometres even three quarters of a second is too long and the green is snuffed out. The red needs a hundred and ten seconds of peace, which it does not get until around two hundred kilometres up. The violet fringe along the bottom of a bright curtain is nitrogen rather than oxygen, and it emits in tens of nanoseconds, so nothing can stop it and it appears wherever the electrons reach.

Look at the middle panels together and it comes out as a rule: green in the body of the curtain, red above it, violet along the bottom edge when the electrons are hard enough to drive down that far. The colour is telling you the altitude, and the altitude is telling you the density.

Hard electrons dig deeper

Where the curtain sits is set by how much energy the incoming electrons carry. A one-kilovolt electron is stopped by the thin air around a hundred and fifty kilometres up, which is red territory, so weak drizzly aurora tend to be red and diffuse. A fifteen-kilovolt electron drives down to about a hundred kilometres, deep in the green, and the hardest ones punch below that and light the nitrogen violet. Drag the energy control and watch the whole curtain slide down its scale and change colour as it goes.

A ring around the wrong pole

Aurora do not sit in a cap over the North Pole. They form a ring, because the field lines that carry the incoming particles are the ones that stretch back into the tail of Earth's magnetic field, and those touch down in a band rather than at the pole. Inside the ring, over the pole itself, the field lines are open and lead nowhere useful, so the sky there is often quieter than it is a thousand kilometres further south.

And the ring is centred on the geomagnetic pole, which currently sits off in northern Canada rather than at the geographic pole. That single fact rearranges the map of who sees aurora. The UK, at 53° north, sits at 55.1° magnetic. Western Canada at the same geographic latitude is at 60.0° magnetic, nearly five degrees better off. Eastern Siberia at 62° north is nine degrees further north than the UK and yet sits at 53.2° magnetic, which is worse. Siberia is a poor place to watch the northern lights and Canada a superb one, for no reason other than where the magnetic pole happens to be. Step through the four places above and watch your marker move in and out of the ring without your latitude changing much at all.

When a storm arrives, the ring swells southward. Quiet nights put its lower edge around 66° magnetic; a strong storm drags it to 55°, which is the latitude of the UK, and a severe one to below 50°.

Why it is always red from down here

The best part, and it ties the two halves of the page together. A curtain is a tall thing, running from about 100 km at its base to 300 or 400 km at the top, and Earth is curved. From a thousand kilometres away the base of the curtain is already below your horizon while the top is still well clear of it. Since the top is the part that glows red and the base is the part that glows green, distance filters the colours.

This is why aurora seen from England are so often reported as a red glow low in the north, while the same display photographed from Scotland is green. You are not seeing a different aurora. You are seeing the top two hundred kilometres of the same one, with the green part hidden behind the curve of the planet. Put yourself inside the ring, then push the latitude down and away from it: the green sinks below the horizon first and the red hangs on above it. Be careful which side of the ring you start on, though. From northern Norway during a decent storm the ring has already slid south of you, so moving south at first brings it closer and lifts the curtain higher before it starts to sink again.

Why the camera sees what you cannot

Put yourself at 53° north and give the model a decent storm, and the readout tells a discouraging story. The green is scraping the horizon or below it, and what reaches twenty degrees up is the red. That already makes UK aurora a marginal business, but there is a second problem, and it is in your eyes rather than in the sky.

Colour vision belongs to the cones in your retina, and cones need a reasonable amount of light. Below that level you switch over to rod vision, which has no colour at all and is most sensitive around 500 nanometres, in the blue-green. Rods are very nearly blind to 630 nanometre red. So a faint red aurora, which is the only part of the display a southern observer can see over the horizon anyway, lands almost exactly where human night vision is worst.

A camera has no such problem. Leave the shutter open for ten seconds and the sensor accumulates what your eye cannot, and it registers red as faithfully as green. This is the whole explanation for a familiar disappointment: photographs from an English garden showing vivid crimson curtains, taken by someone who stood there and saw a vague grey glow and wondered what the fuss was about. They were not imagining the glow and the camera was not lying. The light was real, it was red, and their rods could not see it.

It also explains why the reports change character with latitude. Under the oval the green is overhead and bright enough to work your cones, so people describe vivid colour and movement. From a thousand kilometres south the green is behind the horizon, only the red survives, and the naked-eye account becomes "a pale glow, like a distant town".

Small print

Air density comes from a standard thermosphere table interpolated in log space. Electron penetration uses the usual range law, energy to the power 1.65 in grams per square centimetre, matched against the mass column from above; it puts a 10 keV electron at about 103 km against a textbook 110, which is close enough for the shapes here. The survival of each excited state is the radiative rate divided by the radiative rate plus the collision rate, with the collision coefficients calibrated so that green half-survives at 95 km and red at 220 km, which is what observations show. The three emissions are real spectral lines and are drawn in their true colours: 558 nm green and 630 nm red from atomic oxygen, 428 nm violet from ionised nitrogen. The oval is a circle about the geomagnetic pole at 80.7° north, 72.7° west, with its lower edge at 66.5° minus two degrees per unit of Kp, which is a rule of thumb rather than a model, and the real oval is fatter and pushed further from the pole on the midnight side. Visibility over the horizon is exact geometry for a spherical Earth with no refraction, and the sky panel adds up the light along the whole line of sight as it crosses the five degrees of depth in the ring, which is why a low sight line comes out greener than a steep one.
