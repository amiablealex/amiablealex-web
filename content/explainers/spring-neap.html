Two pullers, one sea

The sun raises tides too — about half the size of the moon's, and on its own clock of exactly twelve hours. Twice a month the two line up and add; twice a month they pull at right angles and cancel. That interference is the whole of spring and neap, and you can watch it happen wave by wave.

Why the sun only manages half

The sun is 27 million times the mass of the moon, and it still loses. Tide-raising force is not gravity but the difference in gravity across Earth, and that difference falls off as the cube of distance rather than the square. The sun is 390 times further away, and 390 cubed is about 59 million. Mass over distance cubed leaves the sun with roughly 46% of the moon's pull — enough to matter a great deal, not enough to take charge.

That single fact is why we have spring and neap at all. A sun at 5% would be a rounding error and a sun at 150% would simply take over. At 46% it is large enough to double the range when it helps and halve it when it does not.

In line, or at right angles

At new moon the moon sits between us and the sun; at full moon it sits opposite. Either way the two sets of bulges point along the same line and their heights add, because a bulge and a bulge on the same axis are the same bulge made bigger. Those are the spring tides — nothing to do with the season, from an old sense of the word meaning to leap up.

At the quarters the moon is 90 degrees from the sun, so the moon's bulge sits where the sun's dip is. Add a peak to a trough and you get something smaller than either. Those are the neaps, and at the equator the range falls to well under half a spring — around 0.34 m against 0.73 m in this model.

Spring and neap are not two kinds of tide. They are the same two waves, beating.

Perigee, and the equinoxes

Two slower rhythms ride on top. The moon's distance runs from about 357,000 to 406,000 km, and because the effect goes as the cube, the moon's pull is some 47% stronger at perigee than at apogee. The phase cycle takes 29.5 days and the distance cycle 27.55, so they drift apart and come back together every seven months or so. When a perigee lands on a new or full moon you get a perigean spring tide, and those are the ones that flood car parks.

The sun contributes its own annual term. Its tide is strongest where it stands overhead, so its contribution to mid-latitude ranges peaks when it crosses the equator — around the equinoxes in March and September. Combine that with a well-placed perigean spring and you have the classic autumn flood tide that every harbourmaster in the North Atlantic has a story about.

The age of the tide

One honest caveat, and an observable one. Spring tides do not arrive on the day of the new or full moon. They arrive a day or two afterwards, by an amount that is fixed for each stretch of coast and is called the age of the tide. The ocean is a heavy thing being driven by a small force, and like anything heavy it lags its driver. The model on this page has no ocean in it and therefore no lag, so its springs land exactly on the syzygy. Yours will not. Everything else about the rhythm — the fortnight, the 46%, the perigean swell — survives the correction intact.

Small print

For each body the equilibrium height is its mass ratio to Earth, times Earth's radius to the fourth, over its distance cubed, times (3cos²ψ − 1)/2, where ψ is the body's angle from your zenith. Amplitudes come out at 0.35 m for the moon and 0.16 m for the sun, which gives an equatorial spring range near a metre and a neap range near 0.4 m. Moon and sun positions come from mean elements with the main periodic terms, good to about a minute of time. The bulges in the first panel are drawn at a fixed exaggeration, the same one for both bodies, so the amber outline really is 46% of the blue one and a spring really does reach further than a neap. Times are local solar time. No ocean, no coastline, no lag.
