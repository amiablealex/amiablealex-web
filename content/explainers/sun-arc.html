What this frame shows

The horizon is fixed at zero degrees, so the arc has to do the moving. Its height at noon is 90° minus the gap between your latitude and the sun's declination, and its width across the sky is the day length. Where your fixed-wave version kept one shape and slid a line through it, this one keeps the line and reshapes the wave, and the two agree exactly on every sunrise and sunset.

The dome adds the thing neither flat chart can show: bearing. At an equinox the sun rises due east anywhere on Earth. In a UK June it rises far in the north-east, cuts a long shallow arc across the south and sets in the north-west, which is why summer evenings stretch out. Near the equator the arc stands almost upright, so the sun drops through the horizon quickly and dusk is brief. Push the latitude to the pole and the arc tips over until it is nearly parallel with the horizon, circling all the way round without touching it.

Things to try

Play the year at 53° N and watch the arc inflate and deflate while the sunrise point slides along the horizon. Drag the viewing height down to 4° to stand almost on the ground, which is closest to what you actually see. Then set the latitude to 90° and the arc becomes a flat ring, rising and falling over the year rather than over the day.

Small print

Bearings run clockwise from north, so 90° is due east. Times are local solar time, with noon when the sun is highest. Sunrise and sunset use the centre of the sun and ignore atmospheric refraction, so real days run a few minutes longer at each end. Dates follow the 2026 calendar and include Earth's slightly elliptical orbit; other tilts keep Earth's orbit. The dome is drawn from outside, as a model rather than a view from your garden, so the sun's arc is never hidden behind you.
