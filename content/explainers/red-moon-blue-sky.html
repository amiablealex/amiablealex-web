The red moon and the blue sky

A moon on the horizon is deep orange; an hour later it is white. Nothing about the moon changed. Air scatters short wavelengths far more strongly than long ones, so a low beam arrives stripped of its blue — and that stripped-out blue, arriving from every other direction at once, is the sky. One process, two results

Air molecules are far smaller than the wavelength of light, and a molecule caught in a passing wave re-radiates it in all directions. How strongly depends viciously on wavelength: the scattered power goes as one over the fourth power, so blue at 450 nanometres is scattered about five and a half times as readily as red at 700. Nothing is absorbed. The light is simply redirected, out of the beam you were looking along and into every other direction.

That single fact answers both questions at once. Look straight at the moon and you see what survived the trip: with the moon overhead the beam crosses one atmosphere's worth of air and loses about a fifth of its blue, which you would never notice. On the horizon it crosses thirty-eight times as much, and less than a thousandth of the blue gets through against a sixth of the red. Look anywhere else and you see the other half of the same transaction: sunlight passing overhead, scattering sideways into your eye, arriving blue. The moon is red because the sky is blue. Set the colour bias slider to zero and both effects die together — a grey sky and a white moon at every height — which is the cleanest proof that they are one phenomenon.

Two details the model shows but the slogan hides. Near the horizon the sky itself turns pale rather than deep blue, because along that long a path even the reds are scattered eventually and the scattering saturates. And the sky is blue rather than violet partly because the sun emits less violet, and mostly because our eyes are far less sensitive to it.

What refraction does instead

The atmosphere does bend light, and it bends it more the lower you look, but bending moves an image rather than colouring it. A moon apparently sitting on the horizon has been lifted about half a degree, which is more than its own width: geometrically it has already set. The lower limb is lifted more than the upper, which is why a rising moon looks squashed, noticeably wider than it is tall. And because the bending is very slightly stronger for blue than for red, the disc carries a thin blue-green fringe on top and a red one below, a minute of arc or so. Stack the right conditions and that upper fringe is the green flash. None of this explains the colour of the disc as a whole, which is settled before the light ever gets bent.

The moon also looks enormous when it rises, and that is not the atmosphere at all. Photograph it low and high with the same lens and the two discs measure the same, to within the couple of percent that Earth's own radius accounts for. The moon illusion happens in the visual system, not in the air.

Small print

Colours are computed rather than chosen: a 5778 K solar spectrum is attenuated by exp(−τ·m), sampled every 5 nm, run through the CIE 1931 colour matching functions and converted to sRGB. The air mass m uses the standard Kasten and Young formula, which gives 38 at the horizon rather than the 1/cos that would give infinity. Optical depth follows a power law in wavelength, pinned to the sea-level Rayleigh value at 550 nm, which matches the exact Rayleigh expression to within a percent across the visible and lets the exponent become a slider. Swatches are normalised to full brightness so you can see the hue; the real dimming is reported separately, and at the horizon it is several magnitudes. The sky colour is single scattering only, with no ozone, no aerosol and no multiple scattering, so it is a touch too saturated overhead. Refraction uses Bennett's formula, and the dispersion is the real one, about four percent between 400 and 700 nm.

The seasons set: the sliding horizon, the sun's real arc, crossing the horizon, the harvest moon, the analemma and eclipse seasons.
