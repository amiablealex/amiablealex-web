Reading the wave

The wave is the sun's journey through one day: midnight at the left edge, noon at the peak, midnight again at the right. In this frame it never changes shape. The horizon is what moves, and it sits at −tan(latitude) × tan(declination), where declination is the latitude at which the sun is directly overhead that day. Over a year, declination swings between plus and minus the axial tilt, so the horizon rises and falls between the two solstice lines.

Where the horizon crosses the wave is sunrise and sunset, exactly. Push the horizon above the peak and the sun never rises; sink it below the troughs and it never sets. The Arctic Circle is the latitude where a solstice line just touches the peak or trough, which is why it sits at 90° minus the tilt. Set the tilt to zero and the horizon locks to the midline everywhere: twelve hours of daylight, every day of the year.

Things to try

Play the year at 53° N and the horizon sweeps between the three lines of your sketch. Drag the latitude towards the equator and those lines squeeze together, or past the Arctic Circle and the solstice lines escape the wave altogether. Then give the UK Uranus's tilt: months of midnight sun, then months of polar night.

Small print

The vertical axis isn't altitude in degrees. It's sin(altitude) ÷ (cos latitude × cos declination), the scaling that keeps the wave fixed; the real arc flattens towards the poles, so the readouts give true altitudes. Times are local solar time, with noon when the sun is highest, so there's no BST, time zone or equation-of-time offset. Sunrise and sunset use the centre of the sun and ignore atmospheric refraction, so real days run a few minutes longer at each end. Dates follow the 2026 calendar and include Earth's slightly elliptical orbit, which is why the seasons aren't quite equal lengths; other tilts keep Earth's orbit. Uranus is tilted about 98°, which gives the same day lengths as 82°.
