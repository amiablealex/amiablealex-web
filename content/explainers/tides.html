Two pullers, one sea. The moon raises a bulge twice its own size on opposite sides of Earth, the sun raises a smaller one of its own, and where those two line up decides whether this week's tides are big or feeble. Then Earth spins through the whole arrangement twice a day.

Why there are two

Gravity weakens with distance, so the moon pulls the near side of Earth harder than the middle, and the middle harder than the far side. That is the whole story, but the conclusion only becomes obvious once you subtract the right thing.

Earth as a body is in free fall towards the moon. Everything in free fall together feels nothing: what an ocean can respond to is only the part of the pull that differs from the pull on Earth as a whole. So take the moon's pull at every point and subtract the pull at Earth's centre. On the near side the pull is stronger than average, and the residual points at the moon. On the far side the pull is weaker than average, and the residual points directly away from the moon. Around the sides the pull is very slightly converging towards the moon, so the residual squeezes inwards. Stretch along one axis, squeeze around the other two, water piles up at both ends. The panel above does that subtraction in front of you, three pictures and two signs: the pull itself is so nearly uniform that it moves nothing, and everything interesting lives in the leftovers. Each arrow there is drawn in two pieces: the grey length every part of Earth shares, and a purple excess magnified about thirty times. On the near side the purple points at the moon, because that water is pulled harder than average. On the far side it points backwards, because that water is pulled less than average and gets left behind. Those purple leftovers, acting for hours across an ocean, are all a tidal bulge is.

The remainder really is tiny. The moon's pull on you is about three hundredths of a millimetre per second squared; what's left after subtracting the average is a thousandth of a millimetre per second squared, about a ten-millionth of Earth's own gravity. It could never lift water by itself. What it can do is act sideways, along the surface, hour after hour across an entire ocean, and sideways is a direction gravity does not resist.

Is the far bulge centrifugal?

You will often read that the far-side bulge is thrown outwards by Earth's swing around the Earth–Moon barycentre. That is not quite a myth, but it is a detour, and it is usually told wrongly. Done carefully, in the frame revolving with the two bodies, the inertial term is exactly the same size and direction at every point of Earth, because in a revolution without rotation every point traces an identical circle. Subtract that uniform term from the moon's non-uniform pull and you get the two bulges. Which is the same arithmetic as subtracting the pull at Earth's centre, because that is precisely what the uniform term equals.

The trouble is what people picture when they hear the word. They imagine something that varies across Earth, or they blame Earth's daily spin, and then they are explaining a real effect with a mechanism that isn't doing the work. Here is the test that settles it: if the moon stopped dead and Earth fell straight towards it, no orbit and no rotation at all, you would get exactly the same two bulges. The stretch comes from the pull being uneven, not from going round anything. Nothing in that three-part picture is orbiting or spinning, and the bulges are there all the same.

Why they come 12 hours 25 minutes apart

The two bulges hold still relative to the moon while Earth turns beneath them, so you pass through two of them each rotation. But the moon has moved on. It takes 24 hours 50 minutes for the moon to come back to your meridian rather than 24 hours, so successive high waters are 12 hours 25 minutes apart and everything drifts about 50 minutes later each day. That is the same fifty minutes that makes moonrise creep later each night on the harvest moon page.

Two a day is the usual case, not the rule. The bulge axis points at the moon, and the moon spends most of the month away from the equator, so the two bulges sit one north and one south of the equator. Sweep round a latitude circle and you cut deeply through one and barely graze the other, which gives unequal highs. Push the latitude slider towards 70° with the moon well off the equator and one of the two daily highs nearly disappears: those places get one tide a day, and plenty of real coastlines do exactly that.

Spring, neap and the moon's distance

The sun raises its own tides at 46% of the moon's strength. Massive as it is, it is four hundred times further away, and tidal pull falls off as the cube of distance rather than the square. At new and full moon the two line up and the ranges add; at the quarters they fight and the range collapses. The dashed pair on the month chart is what the moon would manage on its own: at new and full the solid lines run outside it, and at the quarters they fall inside, because there the sun is working against the moon rather than with it.

The moon's distance matters more than most people expect. It ranges from about 357,000 to 406,000 km, and because the effect goes as the cube, the tide-raising force is about 47% stronger at perigee than at apogee. When a perigee lands on a full or new moon you get the perigean spring tides that coastal forecasters watch for.

What this model does not do

Everything here is the equilibrium tide: the shape the ocean would take if it were deep, global and could respond instantly. Real tides are a forced wave sloshing in basins of awkward shape, and that changes almost everything about the numbers. The equilibrium range is about half a metre; the Bay of Fundy reaches sixteen because its basin happens to resonate near twelve hours. High water does not arrive when the moon is overhead but hours later, by an amount fixed for each port. And the tide does not slide around the globe as a bulge at all: it rotates around amphidromic points where the range falls to nothing. What this model gets right is the rhythm — two a day, 12 hours 25 minutes apart, drifting 50 minutes daily, swelling fortnightly with the phases and monthly with the distance. Every real tide table dances to exactly that beat, then applies its own local correction.

Small print

The equilibrium height is the standard expression: for each body, the mass ratio to Earth times Earth's radius to the fourth over the distance cubed, times (3cos²ψ − 1)/2, where ψ is the body's angle from your zenith. That angle is the same altitude formula the rest of this set uses, so the tide at any moment depends only on how high the moon and sun stand above your horizon. Moon and sun positions come from mean elements with the main periodic terms, good to a minute or so of time. Amplitudes come out at 0.35 m for the moon and 0.16 m for the sun, giving an equatorial spring range near a metre and a neap range near 0.4 m. Times are local solar time. No ocean, no coastline, no lag.
