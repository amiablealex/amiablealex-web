Twice a day, and sometimes once

The two bulges stand still while Earth turns underneath them, so you ride through both of them every day. That much is simple. What isn't is why high water comes fifty minutes later each day, why the two highs are rarely the same size, and why some coasts get only one.

How far the moon swings either side of the equator is not the same every year. It breathes over 18.6 years, and the two extremes have names.

Twelve hours and twenty-five minutes

The bulges hold still relative to the moon while Earth turns beneath them, so one rotation carries you through two of them. But the moon has moved on. It takes 24 hours 50 minutes for the moon to come back to your meridian rather than 24 hours, so successive high waters are 12 hours 25 minutes apart and the whole pattern drifts about fifty minutes later each day. That is the same fifty minutes that makes moonrise creep later each night on the harvest moon page — one fact, two consequences.

Why one high is bigger

Two a day is the usual case, not the rule. The bulge axis points at the moon, and the moon spends nearly all month away from the equator: it swings about 28 degrees either side in a fortnight. So the two bulges sit one north of the equator and one south, and your circle of latitude does not cut them evenly.

The arithmetic is unusually tidy. If you stand at latitude φ and the moon's declination is δ, then when the moon is on your meridian it stands (φ − δ) from your zenith, and twelve hours later, with the moon underfoot, the far bulge's axis is (φ + δ) away. Those two angles are all that separates your two highs. At the equinoxes the moon crosses the equator, δ is nearly zero, the two angles are equal and your tides come in matched pairs. A fortnight later δ is 25 degrees and they are not.

Where the second one disappears

Push the latitude towards 70 degrees with the moon well north and watch the smaller high stop being a high at all. What is left is one rise and one fall a day rather than two of each — a diurnal tide. Plenty of real coastlines do exactly this: much of the Gulf of Mexico, the South China Sea, parts of the Antarctic coast. The rest of the world mostly gets two, and a good many places get two unequal ones, which surveyors call a mixed tide and which is simply this panel with the moon a long way off the equator.

The threshold falls out of the same two angles, and it is a clean one. The second high sits at (φ + δ) from the bulge axis, and the tide's height goes as (3cos²ψ − 1)/2, which stops curving downwards — stops being a maximum there at all — the moment that angle passes ninety degrees. So the second high water survives only while latitude plus declination is under 90°: above 90° minus the moon's declination you get one tide a day. With the moon at 23 degrees that line sits at 67 degrees; over the cycle below it moves between 61 and 72. The derivation assumes the moon holds still at that declination for the day, and it does not quite — it drifts a degree or two — so on the panel the switch lands about a degree lower than the formula says.

The eighteen-year breath

Which raises the obvious question: is 28 degrees the moon's limit? It is, but only sometimes. Earth's axis is tilted 23.44 degrees, and the moon's orbit is tilted another 5.14 degrees to the ecliptic — but those two only add when the moon's orbit crosses the ecliptic at the right place. The crossing points, the nodes, slide backwards right round the ecliptic in 18.61 years, so the two tilts add at one end of that cycle and subtract at the other. The moon's reach breathes between 23.44 + 5.14 = 28.6 degrees and 23.44 − 5.14 = 18.3.

What it does not do is vary with the season. Slide the date through a year and you are watching the same swing thirteen times over; the envelope barely moves. The clock you can feel in the tides is the long one. The wide end is a major lunar standstill — the last was 2024 and 2025, which is why the Callanish stones were in the news — and the narrow end a minor one, due in the early 2030s.

The consequence is worth going to look at. That 90° − δ line sweeps from about 72° at a minor standstill down to 61° at a major one and back again. A ten-degree band of the Arctic, from roughly northern Norway to the top of Greenland, spends part of the cycle with two tides a day and part of it with one. Tromsø, at 69.6°, is inside the band. Nothing about the coast changes; the sky does.

What this model does not do

Everything here is the equilibrium tide: the shape the ocean would take if it were deep, global and could respond instantly. Real tides are a forced wave sloshing in basins of awkward shape, and that changes almost every number. The equilibrium range is about half a metre; the Bay of Fundy reaches sixteen because its basin happens to resonate near twelve hours. High water does not arrive when the moon is overhead but hours later, by an amount fixed for each port. And the tide does not slide around the globe as a bulge at all — it rotates around amphidromic points where the range falls to nothing and turns to nothing but a change in current. What this model gets right is the rhythm: two a day, 12 hours 25 minutes apart, drifting fifty minutes daily, and unequal by exactly as much as the moon is off the equator. Every real tide table dances to that beat and then applies its own local correction.

Two other pages carry the rest of it: why there are two bulges at all, and what the sun adds, which is what makes the range swell and collapse fortnightly.

Small print

Moon positions carry the node's regression, so the standstill cycle in the twenty-year panel is the model's own output rather than something drawn on: it puts the major standstill in 2042 and the minor one in 2033, which is where they are. The threshold latitude is derived rather than fitted: for a fixed declination the second high water stops being a maximum precisely when latitude plus declination reaches 90°. The panels use the real, moving declination, so the switch happens about a degree below that line. The height is the standard equilibrium expression: the moon's mass ratio to Earth, times Earth's radius to the fourth, over the distance cubed, times (3cos²ψ − 1)/2, where ψ is the moon's angle from your zenith. The sun's term is deliberately left out on this page so the daily rhythm is not tangled up with the fortnightly one; put it back on the spring and neap page and every height here grows or shrinks by up to half as much again. Moon positions come from mean elements with the main periodic terms, good to about a minute of time. Times are local solar time. No ocean, no coastline, no lag.
