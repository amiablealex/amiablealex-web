Two tides a day, and sometimes one

The two bulges stand still while Earth turns underneath them, so you ride through both of them every day. That much is simple. What isn't is why high water comes fifty minutes later each day, why the two highs are rarely the same size, and why some coasts get only one.

Twelve hours and twenty-five minutes

The bulges hold still relative to the moon while Earth turns beneath them, so one rotation carries you through two of them. But the moon has moved on. It takes 24 hours 50 minutes for the moon to come back to your meridian rather than 24 hours, so successive high waters are 12 hours 25 minutes apart and the whole pattern drifts about fifty minutes later each day. That is the same fifty minutes that makes moonrise creep later each night on the harvest moon page — one fact, two consequences.

Why one high is bigger

Two a day is the usual case, not the rule. The bulge axis points at the moon, and the moon spends nearly all month away from the equator: it swings about 23 degrees either side of it, from furthest north to furthest south in a fortnight. So the two bulges sit one north of the equator and one south, and your circle of latitude does not cut them evenly.

The arithmetic is unusually tidy. If you stand at latitude φ and the moon's declination is δ, then when the moon is on your meridian it stands (φ − δ) from your zenith, and twelve hours later, with the moon underfoot, the far bulge's axis is (φ + δ) away. Those two angles are all that separates your two highs. Twice a month the moon crosses the equator, δ is nearly zero, the two angles are equal and your tides come in matched pairs. A week later δ is 23 degrees and they are not.

Where the second one disappears

Push the latitude towards 70 degrees with the moon well north and watch the smaller high stop being a high at all. What is left is one rise and one fall a day rather than two of each — a diurnal tide. Plenty of real coastlines do exactly this: much of the Gulf of Mexico, the South China Sea, parts of the Antarctic coast. The rest of the world mostly gets two, and a good many places get two unequal ones, which surveyors call a mixed tide and which is simply this panel with the moon a long way off the equator.

The threshold falls out of the same two angles, and it is a clean one. The second high sits at (φ + δ) from the bulge axis, and the tide's height goes as (3cos²ψ − 1)/2, which stops curving downwards — stops being a maximum there at all — the moment that angle passes ninety degrees. So the second high water survives only while latitude plus declination is under 90°: above 90° minus the moon's declination you get one tide a day. With the moon at its furthest, 23.4 degrees, that line sits at 66.6 degrees: the Arctic Circle, and for the same reason, since the Arctic Circle is ninety degrees minus the sun's own furthest. It is also exactly the latitude beyond which a moon that far north never sets, which is no coincidence either. The second high water arrives as the moon passes beneath you, and a moon that never drops below your horizon never gets far enough beneath you to bring it. The derivation assumes the moon holds still at that declination for the day, and it does not quite — it drifts a degree or two — so on the panel the switch lands about a degree lower than the formula says.

The real moon wanders further

This page sets the moon on the ecliptic, the sun's own road, so it reaches 23.4 degrees either side of the equator every month of every year. The real moon's orbit is tilted another five degrees, and how far that carries it breathes over 18.6 years, from 28.6 degrees at a major lunar standstill to 18.3 at a minor one; that has a page of its own. For tides it means the one-a-day line is not pinned to the Arctic Circle but wanders between about 61 and 72 degrees over the cycle. A band of the north from about Trondheim to the North Cape, with Iceland in the middle of it, spends part of the cycle with two tides a day and part of it with one. Tromsø, at 69.6°, is inside it. Nothing about the coast changes; the sky does.

What this model does not do

Everything here is the equilibrium tide: the shape the ocean would take if it were deep, global and could respond instantly. Real tides are a forced wave sloshing in basins of awkward shape, and that changes almost every number. The equilibrium range is about half a metre; the Bay of Fundy reaches sixteen because its basin happens to resonate near twelve hours. High water does not arrive when the moon is overhead but hours later, by an amount fixed for each port. And the tide does not slide around the globe as a bulge at all — it rotates around amphidromic points where the range falls to nothing and turns to nothing but a change in current. What this model gets right is the rhythm: two a day, 12 hours 25 minutes apart, drifting fifty minutes daily, and unequal by exactly as much as the moon is off the equator. Every real tide table dances to that beat and then applies its own local correction.

Two other pages carry the rest of it: why there are two bulges at all, and what the sun adds, which is what makes the range swell and collapse fortnightly.

Small print

The moon here rides exactly on the ecliptic: its place along it and its distance are real, but its five-degree tilt is left out, so it reaches 23.44° either side of the equator every month. The real moon sits up to five degrees north or south of where it is drawn, which moves the threshold latitude about but shifts the times of high water by ten minutes at most; the tilt and its 18.6-year cycle are on the standstills page. The threshold latitude is derived rather than fitted: for a fixed declination the second high water stops being a maximum precisely when latitude plus declination reaches 90°. The panels use the real, moving declination, so the switch happens about a degree below that line. The height is the standard equilibrium expression: the moon's mass ratio to Earth, times Earth's radius to the fourth, over the distance cubed, times (3cos²ψ − 1)/2, where ψ is the moon's angle from your zenith. The sun's term is deliberately left out on this page so the daily rhythm is not tangled up with the fortnightly one; put it back on the spring and neap page and every height here grows or shrinks by up to half as much again. Moon positions come from mean elements with the main periodic terms, good to about a minute of time. Times are local solar time. No ocean, no coastline, no lag.
