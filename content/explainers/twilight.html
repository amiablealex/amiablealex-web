Earth's own shadow

On a clear evening, turn your back on the sunset. Low in the east there is a dark blue-grey band with a softer, brighter one above it, and the dark band climbs as you watch. That band is the shadow of the planet you are standing on, twelve and a half thousand kilometres of it, thrown onto our own air. Almost nobody looks.

A shadow you can measure

Earth blocks sunlight, so it casts a shadow, and that shadow is a cone stretching a million and a half kilometres out into space. You are standing inside the near end of it from sunset to sunrise. What makes it visible is that our own air is in the way: the shadow falls across the atmosphere to your east, and the air inside it has nothing to scatter towards you while the air above it still catches the sun.

So the dark band has a top edge, and that edge is a real, measurable thing. It first appears at the eastern horizon at the moment of sunset and climbs as the sun sinks further. The climb starts fast, a degree or two of sky for each degree the sun drops, then accelerates sharply: by the time the sun is six degrees down the shadow's edge has reached about ten degrees of elevation, and shortly after that it sweeps over the zenith and the whole sky is inside it. Watch the readout as you drag the sun down.

Two details worth knowing before you go outside. The edge is curved, gently, because it is the shadow of a sphere, and on a wide clear horizon over the sea you can see the curve. And the band is bluish-grey rather than black, because the air inside the shadow is still lit by light scattered down from the sunlit air above it. What you are seeing is not the absence of light but a deficit.

The bright band above it

Directly above the shadow sits a brighter, softer band, usually called the Belt of Venus. It is the lowest air still in sunlight, and the sunlight reaching it has grazed the whole thickness of the atmosphere on its way over the horizon, so it has been stripped of its blue exactly as described on the red moon page. That is why the belt is the warmest part of the twilight sky and why photographs show it as rose or salmon.

An honest note about this page's colours. The model here follows sunlight scattering once, from the sun to a parcel of air to your eye, and that is enough to place the shadow, the bright band and the brightness collapse correctly. It is not enough to reproduce the belt's full rosiness, which depends on light that has bounced more than once before it reaches you. So the band comes out bright and barely warm rather than pink. Everything else on the page is what the model gives, and I would rather show you that than tint it by hand and call it physics.

Why the blue hour is blue, and it isn't what you think

Here is the part that surprised me. The daytime sky is blue because air scatters short wavelengths more strongly. That explanation does not work for twilight. Twilight light has crossed an enormous thickness of air on its way past the horizon, which strips out the blue and should leave the sky yellow-orange, the way it leaves a low moon orange.

So take the ozone out. The second slider does exactly that, and at a sun a degree or two down the sky turns cream and tan, which is what a purely scattering atmosphere would give you. Put it back to the 300 Dobson units really up there and it goes blue again. The effect is strongest in the first few degrees: drag the sun deeper and the ozone slider stops mattering, because by then the light reaching you has come from air above the layer altogether. Ozone sits in a layer around twenty-five kilometres up and absorbs broadly across the orange and red through what is called the Chappuis band. The slider runs from nothing to about 460 Dobson units, which covers the real range: the global average is near 300, the tropics run lower, and the Antarctic hole drops below 120, which is the setting worth trying. It hardly matters when the sun is high and the path through the layer is short. In twilight the light travels nearly horizontally through that layer for hundreds of kilometres, the absorption becomes severe, and what survives is blue. The blue hour is an absorption colour, not a scattering colour, and it is a different blue from the daytime sky for a genuinely different reason.

Three twilights, and the nights we never get

The stages are conventions with real content behind them. Civil twilight ends when the sun is six degrees down and you can no longer comfortably read outside. Nautical ends at twelve, which is roughly when the sea horizon stops being sharp enough to take a star sight against. Astronomical ends at eighteen, the point where the last of the sunlit air has dropped below the horizon and the sky stops getting darker at all.

How long that takes depends on the angle at which the sun's path meets the horizon, which is the same geometry as crossing the horizon. At the equator the sun drops almost vertically and the whole sequence is over in about seventy minutes. At 53° north in June it never finishes: the sun only reaches about fourteen degrees below the horizon at midnight, so from 16 May to 27 July there is no astronomical night at all in the UK, only a sky that thins to nautical twilight and brightens again. Drag the latitude on the last panel and watch the black band close up entirely.

Small print

Sky colours come from a single-scattering model: for each direction, the line of sight is walked out to 120 km in 2 km steps, each parcel is tested against the geometric terminator with an allowance for refraction, and the sunlight reaching it is attenuated by the Rayleigh column along its grazing path, computed from the tangent height with the Chapman formula, plus ozone absorption through a layer centred at 22 km with a 300 Dobson unit column. The surviving spectrum is scattered towards the eye in proportion to the Rayleigh coefficient, summed along the ray, and converted through the CIE 1931 functions to sRGB. Brightness is shown relative to the moment of sunset. Single scattering overstates how fast the light goes after nautical twilight, because by then most of what is left has bounced more than once, so treat the far end of that curve as a lower bound. Multiple scattering is not modelled, which is the limitation described above; the faint glow inside the shadow is a stand-in for it, set at a few per cent of the sunlit sky. Twilight timings use the sun's declination and hour angle without refraction at the horizon, so real-world times run a minute or two later.
