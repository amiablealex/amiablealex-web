Crossing the horizon

A sunset takes longer at a solstice than at an equinox. Not because the sun's path tilts over — that tilt is fixed by your latitude and never changes — but because of where the horizon cuts across it.

The two angles

The sun's daily path is a circle, and that circle's plane stands square to Earth's axis. The axis points at the pole star, which sits at an altitude equal to your latitude, so the plane meets the horizon at 90° minus your latitude: 37° at 53° N, every day of the year. Sliding the date moves the circle along the axis, which cannot tip it. That's why the hoop's lean looks unchanged as you scrub through the seasons.

What moves is the horizon line across the face of the circle. At an equinox it passes through the centre, a diameter, and the sun meets it square on. At a solstice it cuts a chord nearer the edge, where the sun's travel is already swinging sideways, and the angle within the plane drops to about 55°. The angle you actually see is the two combined: the sine of the crossing angle is the sine of the plane's tilt times the sine of the in-plane angle.

Two limits check the rule. At the equator the plane stands vertical and the chord is always a diameter, so the sun plunges at 90° on every date of the year, which is why tropical dusk is famously abrupt. At the Arctic Circle in June the chord shrinks to a single point of contact, the angle falls to zero, and the sun grazes the horizon without crossing it.

Small print

Everything is geometric: the centre of the sun, no atmospheric refraction, which in reality lifts the sun by about half a degree near the horizon and stretches sunset a little further. Sunset duration is the time for one solar diameter, 0.53°, to pass the horizon at the current drop rate. Civil twilight is the sun's centre falling from the horizon to 6° below it, and it never ends at all when the sun stops short of that depth, which is the UK's midsummer half-light. Times are local solar time. Dates follow the 2026 calendar and include Earth's slightly elliptical orbit; other tilts keep Earth's orbit.
