The harvest moon

Around the September full moon, moonrise creeps later by a quarter of an hour a night instead of the usual fifty minutes, and the harvest light holds for a week. It comes from the same place as the moon's height through the year: where the moon sits on the ecliptic, which is the sun's longitude plus the phase.

One rule behind all of it

The moon travels the same road as the sun, the ecliptic, but about thirteen times faster: it laps the whole circuit in a month rather than a year. Its phase is simply how far round that road it sits from the sun. New moon shares the sun's longitude, full moon is exactly opposite, the quarters are ninety degrees either side. So the moon's declination, and with it the height of its arc, is the declination of the point the sun will reach, or last reached, a quarter, half or three quarters of a year away.

That single rule gives you the whole table. A full moon in December sits where the sun sits in June, so it climbs to 60° at 53° N and stays up for nearly seventeen hours. A full moon in June copies the December sun and barely clears 13°. New moon always copies today's sun, which is why the December new moon is the shallowest of them all. At an equinox the full and new moons are mid-arc, and it's the quarter moons that take the extremes: in September the last quarter rides high and the first quarter scrapes the horizon, and in March they swap.

Why the harvest moon bunches up

Each night the moon has moved about 13° further east along the ecliptic, so the sky has to turn that much further to lift it, which normally costs around fifty minutes. Two things cut the bill in September. The first is the angle: near the equinox points the ecliptic lies shallow against the eastern horizon in the evening, so those 13° run mostly sideways along the horizon rather than down beneath it. Little vertical drop means little extra rotation. The second is that the moon's declination is climbing fast there, several degrees a night, and a moon further north rises earlier for anyone in the north, which claws back more time still.

Both terms scale with latitude, which is why this is a northern-harvest story rather than a tropical one. At the equator the delay sits near forty-five minutes all year. At 53° N the September full moon comes in around a quarter of an hour. Push the slider to 65° N and it reaches zero, and beyond that the moon rises earlier each night. Set the phase to new and the whole pattern slides to March, because what matters is where the moon is on the ecliptic, not what it's lit like. The southern hemisphere gets its harvest moon in March for the same reason, six months out of step.

Small print

The moon here is idealised: it runs exactly along the ecliptic at a steady 13.18° a day, so the phase advances 12.19° a day and a lunation takes 29.53 days. The real moon's orbit is tilted 5.1°, which lifts or lowers its declination by up to that much and swings on an 18.6-year cycle, so real harvest moons are a little better or worse than these figures; its orbit is also elliptical, so it runs fast and slow through the month. Rise and set are geometric, for the centre of the moon, with no refraction and no parallax, both of which push real moonrise a few minutes earlier. Times are local solar time. The phase drawing assumes a northern-hemisphere view; from the south it appears flipped. Dates follow the 2026 calendar for the sun's position, and any phase can be set on any date, which in reality only happens on the dates it happens.
