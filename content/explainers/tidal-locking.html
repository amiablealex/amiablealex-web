One face, always

A tide is not an ocean thing. It is what happens whenever gravity pulls harder on your near side than your far side, and rock stretches as readily as water. Earth kneads the moon about twenty-two times harder than the moon kneads us, and over tens of millions of years that kneading did something remarkable: it dragged the moon's spin to exactly one turn per orbit and pinned it there. Nobody saw the far side until 1959, and it was never a coincidence.

A tide is not an ocean

The word misleads. A tide is what is left of another body's gravity once you subtract the average pull on you as a whole: a stretch along the line between you, squeezed in at the sides, as the tides page works through. Water shows it best because water flows, but the effect does not care what it is acting on. Earth's own crust rises and falls about 30 cm twice a day. The moon flexes too, by about 10 cm, even though it is solid rock throughout. Jupiter kneads Io so hard that the friction keeps it molten and makes it the most volcanic body in the solar system, with no ocean involved anywhere. Get close enough to a large body and the stretch exceeds what your own gravity can hold together, which is why Saturn has rings instead of one more moon.

So when this page says Earth raises a tide on the moon, it means the moon is being pulled into a slightly egg shape, permanently, pointing at us. That egg is the handle by which everything else happens.

It turns. That's the point.

The commonest thing said about the moon is that it doesn't rotate, and it is exactly backwards. Set the spin control to zero and watch: a moon that genuinely didn't turn would show us every side of itself over a month, because it would hold one face towards the distant stars while we looked at it from every angle in turn. To keep one face towards Earth it has to turn once for every lap, and it does. 27.32 days to go round, 27.32 days to turn. Those two numbers being equal is not a coincidence here, and it is not a coincidence for any of the large moons in the solar system either. It is the end state of a process.

How a bulge becomes a brake

From the tides page: a nearby body does not pull on you evenly, and what is left after you subtract the average stretches you along the line towards it. Earth does that to the moon about twenty-two times more strongly than the moon does it to us, because Earth is eighty-one times heavier.

Suppose the young moon spun faster than it orbited. Its rocky bulge tries to point at Earth, but the moon's own rotation keeps carrying that bulge past the line, and rock takes time to relax, so the bulge ends up sitting slightly ahead of where it should be. Now Earth has something off-axis to grab. Its pull on the leading bulge is a little stronger than on the trailing one, because it is nearer, and that difference is a twist acting against the spin. Grind away for long enough and the spin slows until the bulge is no longer carried past the line, which happens precisely when the spin matches the orbit. At that moment the bulge points straight at Earth, there is nothing off-axis left to pull on, and the twist switches off.

Slide the spin control through 1 and watch the lag flip sides in the left-hand panel. Faster than its orbit, and the twist is a brake. Slower, and the twist is an accelerator.

Not a coincidence: a destination

This is the part that looks like a fluke and isn't. It seems extraordinary that the moon's day and its month should be the same length to five decimal places, as though someone had set a dial. But look at the twist curve. It is negative everywhere above one turn per orbit and positive everywhere below it, which means a moon turning too fast gets braked towards one turn per orbit, and a moon turning too slowly gets hurried towards one turn per orbit. Every spin rate on that axis is being pushed at the same target, from whichever side it happens to start.

And the push weakens as it gets there, because the lag that produces it is itself caused by the mismatch. So a moon does not overshoot and oscillate: it slides in and stops. Press "let the tides act" from any spin you like and watch it arrive, slowing as it closes in. A rate like that, which pulls its neighbourhood in towards itself and then holds, is what physics calls a stable equilibrium, and it means the outcome was not lucky but inevitable — given enough time, and the moon has had four and a half billion years of it.

The same argument explains the roll call. Every large moon of Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune keeps one face to its planet. Pluto and Charon have gone all the way and locked to each other. These are not coincidences either; they are the same destination reached by different bodies at different speeds. What varies is how long it takes, and that depends mostly on distance, since the twist falls off as the sixth power of separation. Our moon got there in perhaps ten to a hundred million years, a blink. Earth, feeling a twist twenty-two times weaker and carrying eighty-one times the mass to slow, has not finished and never will.

Why Mercury didn't do the same

One caveat to all that inevitability. Mercury turns three times for every two orbits rather than once per orbit, and the reason is its lopsided path around the sun. Tidal torque falls off as the cube of distance, so almost all of it is delivered in the short stretch around closest approach, and what matters is the spin rate there rather than the average. On an orbit as eccentric as Mercury's, three turns per two orbits is stable too: it presents the same face to the sun at every other perihelion. Set the spin control to 1.5 and you can watch that pattern repeat every second lap. So the target is not always exactly one turn per orbit: what is always true is that there is a target, and that tides deliver you to it.

The wobble in the lock

The lock is only exact on average. The moon turns at a perfectly steady rate but it does not orbit at a steady rate: like everything on an ellipse it hurries at perigee and dawdles at apogee. Its spin cannot hurry to match, so the face it presents rocks east and west by about six degrees over a month and we peer a little way around each limb in turn. Its spin axis is also tilted nearly seven degrees to its orbit, so we see alternately over the north pole and the south. The map panel adds those up and lands near 58% of the surface ever visible, against 50% for a perfect lock. The usual quoted figure is 59%, the extra coming from the moon's own slight physical wobble and from Earth being 12,700 km across, so that an observer watching the moon rise and one watching it set are looking from measurably different angles.

It is happening to us, just slowly

The moon is doing to Earth exactly what Earth did to the moon, and it is winning slowly. Our tidal bulge is dragged ahead of the moon by our fast rotation, the moon pulls back on it, and our spin bleeds away at about 1.7 milliseconds per century. That angular momentum is not destroyed. The same off-axis bulge pulls the moon forwards along its orbit, which lifts it into a wider one, and the retroreflectors left by Apollo let us measure the retreat directly: 3.8 cm a year.

The last panel is the bookkeeping. Fix the total angular momentum of the system and the moon's distance alone decides the length of our day, with no model of friction needed. When the moon sat at four Earth radii, freshly formed, the day was five hours. At 58 radii it was 22 hours, which is what fossil tidal sediments from 620 million years ago record. Today it is 60.3 radii and 23.93 hours of actual rotation. The two curves converge at about 87 radii, where day and month both become 47 present-day days and the whole thing stops, with Earth keeping one face towards the moon as Pluto and Charon already do to each other. That would take roughly fifty billion years, and the sun will have swollen into a red giant and ended the argument some forty billion years earlier.

Small print

The lag angle in the middle panel is schematic: the real one is small, a couple of degrees, and is set by how readily rock and water dissipate energy rather than by any tidy formula. Everything else is computed. The orbit uses the moon's real eccentricity of 0.0549 and its 6.69° tilt to the orbital plane, with the east–west and north–south wobbles running on their true and slightly different periods of 27.55 and 27.21 days, which is why the trace never quite repeats. Day length against distance comes from conserving the system's total angular momentum with Earth's moment of inertia at 0.3307 MR², and it reproduces the textbook five-hour day at formation and 47 days at mutual locking without being fitted to either. "Day" here means one rotation of Earth, 23.93 hours today; our 24-hour clock day is that plus the few minutes our own travel around the sun adds. The timings are the uncertain part: today's retreat rate is unusually fast because the present ocean basins resonate well, so running it backwards naively puts the moon at Earth's surface 1.5 billion years ago, which is wrong by about three billion.
